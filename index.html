The premise

The setting: CyberCity

CyberCity is a coastal city of roughly 400,000 people. It has a container port that handles a third of the region's freight, a public hospital, a water and wastewater utility, a municipal identity and services portal that every resident uses to pay taxes and book appointments, and about two thousand small companies that supply all of the above.

It is day two of an incident. Nobody yet knows how it started. Scheduling at the hospital is degraded, the port has fallen back to paper manifests, login attempts to the city portal are spiking from places they shouldn't be, and the city's communications team has published two statements that contradict each other. The people responding are ordinary, tired, and outnumbered — a four-person IT team at the hospital, one security engineer at the port, a policy officer at city hall who has never handled a breach.

All three tracks share this setting. Whatever you build, build it for someone in CyberCity, and be able to name them. The city is fictional on purpose — so that nothing is tested against a real system, and so that every team designs for the same constraints. The failure modes are ordinary ones you can find in any incident report from the last five years.

Choose one

Three tracks

Pick the track your project belongs to at the Tuesday session. You can switch up to Thursday 18:00. Tracks exist so that judging compares like with like and so the showcase has a shape — they are not three separate competitions, and the same scoring sheet applies to all of them.

Track A

Resilience Under Attack

How does a service people depend on keep running while it is under attack — and get back on its feet afterwards?

Infrastructure and the systems behind it: hospital, port, water, cloud and edge, industrial sensors, and the supply chain feeding all of them. Prevention, detection, forensics, response, recovery, and secure delivery.

  • Fits here
  • Anomaly detection on the water utility's sensors
  • Blast-radius containment for the port's cloud workloads
  • Forensic triage for a four-person hospital IT team
  • Which of 2,000 suppliers can reach the hospital network?
  • A pipeline gate that blocks the deploy that caused this
Track B

Secure & Trustworthy AI

Can AI carry real defensive weight — and can we trust the AI already running inside the city?

Both directions count. AI for security: red- and blue-teaming with models, triage, correlation, automation for teams too small to keep up. Security of AI: model and data integrity, prompt injection, poisoning, MLOps, provenance.

  • Fits here
  • Model-driven red-team against a service, then the control that stops it
  • Prompt-injection defence for the city's support assistant
  • Alert triage that explains why it deprioritised something
  • Integrity checks across an ML supply chain
  • Federated detection across operators who can't share data
Track C

Defending the Digital Citizen

What protects the 400,000 people who never chose to be part of this?

Identity, privacy, consent, safety and trust at human scale — privacy-enhancing technologies, and the policy, communication and interface design that decides whether protection actually reaches anyone.

  • Fits here
  • Publish incident statistics without exposing individuals
  • Account recovery for residents locked out of the city portal
  • A breach notification residents can actually act on
  • Consent and data minimisation for city services
  • Making post-quantum risk legible to non-specialists

Expectations

What you build

One capability, working. Not a tour of the system.

The whole project is judged, but five minutes is nowhere near enough to show all of it. Before you plan the presentation, write one sentence: "Our project lets [who] [do what] when [what happens]." If that sentence contains the word "and", cut it in half and pick the better half — that half is what you demo. Groups that choose one thing and show it working land. Groups that tour every feature spend Saturday morning demoing a login screen.

Required in every presentation

The adversary moment. At some point in your five minutes, show the threat happening and show your prototype responding to it. Trigger the attack, feed in the malicious input, play the scenario forward. A description is not a demo. If your prototype is a policy or design artefact rather than software, walk us through one concrete incident with the artefact in play, minute by minute.

What counts as a prototype

Anything demonstrable. Working software is the obvious case, but a clickable interface with a scripted backend, a tested playbook, a hardware rig, or a well-run tabletop exercise all qualify — provided we can watch it work rather than hear it described. Non-code prototypes are judged on exactly the same criteria and have exactly the same chance of winning.

What you hand in on Saturday

  1. A live demo — running on your machine, plus a recording as backup.
  2. A five-minute presentation, followed by three minutes of questions from the jury.
  3. A one-page team sheet: the problem, who in CyberCity you're defending, what you built, what you'd do next, and which AI tools you used.
  4. A link to your repository, files, or documents.
Encouraged

Continuing your working group project

Your working group project is the natural place to start, and we would far rather you took it further than abandoned it for something you can only half-build in a week. Continuing is the expected route, not a fallback.

How this is scored

The whole project is judged — everything you present on Saturday, not only the part you added during the hackathon week. There is no baseline to declare and nothing to measure against. Bring the strongest version of the work you have, and show us what it does.

Every group arrives with a project behind it, so this puts everyone on the same footing. What it changes is where the week is best spent: on the capability that is still missing, the demo that proves the thing works, and the five minutes that explain why any of it matters. Those are the three places a good project wins or loses on Saturday.

Starting something entirely new instead is allowed. Tell us at the kick-off if you do — same scoring sheet, and the jury will know what you took on.

Logistics

Teams

  • Your working group is your hackathon team by default. You don't need to do anything to keep it.
  • Re-forming is possible, with approval. If you want to leave, merge or split a group, talk to us by Thursday 17 September, 12:00 and we'll agree it with you and the groups affected.
  • Two to five people. Solo entries aren't accepted — presenting as a team is part of what's assessed.
  • Every member must be present on Saturday to be named on an award.
Wednesday evening

The Exchange

One hour, mid-week, where every team puts its hardest current problem on the wall and every other team helps solve it. It is the opposite of a competition, and it happens two days before one — which is exactly the point. Ten teams in a room have solved more problems than any of you have met.

Before you arrive — by Wed 17:00

Each team posts one challenge, in two sentences maximum. Make it a real blocker, not a polite one. "We can't get the model to run on the conference wifi" is a better post than "we are exploring approaches to detection." Vague problems get vague help.

Attendance is expected but not scored. Nobody loses anything by missing it, except the answers.

Assessment

How you'll be judged

Four criteria, scored 1–5 by each judge and weighted. The same sheet is used in all three tracks, and your whole project is in scope. The weighting leans toward the idea on purpose: what separates projects at this level is rarely the code — it is whether you found a real problem and thought about it harder than anyone else in the room.

Idea & innovation

40/100

Is the problem real, and is the angle on it fresh?

  • A specific defender in CyberCity, not a generic "users"
  • An insight the obvious solution misses
  • Honest about what already exists and why it isn't enough
  • Ambition — the interesting failure scores above the safe success

Implementation & demo

25/100

Does the thing actually work?

  • The adversary moment lands — we saw the threat and the response
  • How much of the project is real and running, rather than described
  • Sensible technical choices for the problem and the time available
  • Honesty about what is stubbed, faked, or hard-coded — say so and lose nothing

Presentation

20/100

Could a hospital IT manager follow it?

  • Clear structure: problem, who it hurts, what you built, what you saw
  • Kept to five minutes without rushing the demo
  • Answers the jury's actual question
  • More than one voice from the team

Impact & feasibility

15/100

If this existed on Monday, would anyone use it?

  • Fits the constraints of the people meant to run it
  • Realistic about deployment, cost, data, and legal reality
  • A credible next step, not a roadmap to a unicorn
  • Considers who else it affects — including who it could harm

The arithmetic. Each judge scores every criterion 1–5. A criterion's points are score × weight ÷ 5, so a 4 on Idea is worth 32. Judge totals are averaged. Ties break on Idea & innovation first, then on helper points from the Exchange, then by jury discussion.

Non-negotiable

House rules

Legal and ethical boundaries

  • Nothing is tested against systems you do not own. No scanning, probing or traffic capture on the venue network, the university's infrastructure, conference attendees' devices, or any third party. Targets must be your own lab, a container you built, or a sandbox we provide. This includes the AI red-teaming work in Track B: model-driven attacks run against your own deployed target, never a live site.
  • No real personal data. Use synthetic or properly licensed public datasets. If your idea needs personal data, generate it. Anything involving real people's data must be lawful under GDPR and you must be able to say why it is.
  • Offensive tooling may be built and demonstrated only against your own targets, and only where the defensive purpose is clear.
  • A breach of either rule ends your participation. This is the one place we have no flexibility.

Tools and reuse

  • AI assistants are allowed and expected. This is how the work is done now. Declare on your team sheet what you used them for, in one line. Nobody loses points for it; teams that hide it and then can't explain their own code lose points in Q&A.
  • Open-source libraries, frameworks, models and public datasets are all fair game. Standing on shoulders is not cheating. Passing off someone else's project as your build is.
  • Credit anything substantial you didn't write, on the team sheet.

Conduct

  • The conference code of conduct applies throughout.
  • Mentors help everyone equally and don't write your code.
  • Help other teams at the Exchange in good faith. Points go to the answers that helped, and everyone in the room can tell the difference.
Asked in advance

Questions

Do we have to write code?

No. You have to demonstrate something. A tested playbook, a designed flow, or a run tabletop exercise competes on equal terms with software — Track C in particular expects some of this. What you cannot do is describe an idea and call it a prototype.

Can we just continue our working group project?

Yes — that is the expected route, and most of you will. The whole project is judged, not only what you add during the hackathon week, so bring the strongest version of it. Nothing to declare, no baseline to measure.

Can we switch tracks?

Yes, until Thursday 18:00. After that the presentation order is fixed.

Our project doesn't obviously fit any track.

Check the topic table above first — every conference topic has a home there. If it still doesn't fit, tell us at the Tuesday session and we'll place it with you rather than making you bend the project.

When are we supposed to build, with the conference running?

Your own hours, in whatever time you have. That's why Saturday morning includes eighty minutes of finishing sprint, and why the scope rule applies to what you demo rather than to what you build. Nobody expects you to skip the conference.

What if the demo breaks during our presentation?

Play the backup recording and keep talking. Judges have seen live demos fail since the invention of live demos; the recording costs you nothing. Not having one costs you your Implementation score.

Is the Exchange compulsory?

Expected, not scored. Nobody loses points for missing it — you just lose the answers, and the chance at the Best Collaborators certificate.
